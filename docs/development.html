Features and Development

Water Element

The water element involves several components: vertex displacement, reflection/refraction, Fresnel effects and normals, dissolving, particle systems, and interaction with the environment. Vertex displacement uses 3D simplex noise for tiling on a sphere without artefacts, scaling the world position of the vertex to generate dynamic surface movement. Reflections and refractions, key to water realism [1], are approximated through distorted scene texture sampling, avoiding the computational cost of real-time ray tracing. Fresnel effects enhance rim lighting, another effect key to perceived realism of water, and normal mapping adds some extra surface texture. To get this working with the interaction, water is handled as a 3D object emitted by a particle system, where particles represent blobs of water and droplets. In order to enhance realism, we handle water dripping onto the floor as a render texture, where each hand's projected coordinate "paints" the render texture. This texture is used to calculate normals, modulate albedo, apply Fresnel effects, and dynamically change surface properties, including wetness and smoothness. Noise shapes the outlines of the puddles on the floor, with Gaussian blur and fading over time adding realism. This approach of approximating the effects of water in a porous surface (making it darker) and surface water puddles is inspired by Jensen et al [2]. An additional effect added are caustics, which are approximated by a Voronoi texture projected onto the scene.

Lightning Element

The lightning element was created using a tessellation shader on the GPU in HLSL to generate detailed lightning-style meshes, with a Bezier curve function applied to simulate realistic arcs and splits. The technique used was based on a number of academic sources [3][4] as well as sources from the industry taking inspiration from Nvidia's own proposed technique [5]. However, several challenges arose during development. Transparency and alpha clipping were particularly problematic, as achieving smooth blending without visual artifacts required fine-tuning and often led to inconsistent results. Additionally, the tessellation approach introduced compatibility issues on GPUs with limited support, necessitating extra testing and optimizations. Unity's lack of native support for line tessellation further complicated the process, requiring the use of alternative techniques, such as approximating line geometry with quads, which added complexity and performance overhead. These issues highlighted limitations in both hardware and software that had to be carefully navigated throughout development.

Fire Element

The fire element consists of three major interaction features, namely: Fire hands, flamethrower and meteor. Each of these features are triggered by the positional data of a users' hands. During the initial concept phase, I was looking into how volumetric fire could be implemented [6], due to performance issues, it was necessary to switch over to particle-based systems instead using custom shaders made in ShaderGraph. In order to create the fire hand and flamethrower effect, particle effects using texture sheets drawn in Photoshop were added, with procedural and simple noise being lerped together to create a layered noise effect, giving the effect of living flames as particles would inherit a random appearance [7]. Flame particles utilize hand positional data for the hands to track and follow the hands and to enable/disable the flames. For the flamethrower, this was in the end done by taking the average positional data and normalized rotation of the hands and emitting the flames with adjustments to the particle settings to enable collisional data to be received from ray casting to burnable objects. To handle burnable objects a stepwise burn texture to the object was added, then “dissolves” the texture by stepwise adjusting the albedo along the mesh to 0 depending on the amount of collisions to objects marked with the “Burnable” tag that use this custom burn shader. Finally I modelled a meteor in Blender, then added fire and smoke particle effects similarly to the above fire shader, then instantiated the object once a gesture has been met i.e. keep hand raised and then lower hand. This object is transformed from its start point towards a plane which replaces the meteor with a simple point of light explosion particle effect.

Wind Element

The wind effect was created as a procedural system combining volume rendering and dynamic parameter tweaking to align with the project's stylized aesthetic. During the prototyping phase, I explored various methods to represent the wind's movement visually. Leveraging ideas from procedural noise and ray marching techniques (as described by Scratchapixel [9]), I generated a volumetric effect using a noise function. By scaling vector components of the noise sample position, I enhanced the visual complexity and shape of the wind, giving it a more organic feel. I also exposed variables from the C# script, enabling other team members to synchronize the wind's state with environmental sounds and additional effects. This integration allowed the wind dome to move seamlessly with the player. Finally, I adjusted particle colors and densities to ensure the effect matched the surrounding environment while maintaining performance. Resources like the Real-Time Rendering [8] book, Scratchapixel's practical examples [9], and Guerrilla SIGGRAPH course [10] helped me design a wind dome effect that balances performance and visual quality, demonstrating the adaptability of volume rendering in gaming contexts.

Motion Capture

The motion capture system was built using MediaPipe integrated into Unity via the MediaPipe for Unity package. We utilised the distance between tracked points to measure limb extension, which worked well for larger movements but had less precision for smaller limbs like fingers. This limitation influenced the game design, leading to a focus on full-body poses and easily recognizable landmarks, such as hands raised above the head. To accommodate player movement, including jumping and floor interactions, we implemented manual calibration to establish the player's position relative to the ground. This calibration process ensured a consistent experience but required additional effort to fine-tune for varying environments and player setups.

Environmental Effects and Soundscape

The rain and lightning effects were all created using Unity's particle systems. For the rain, it spawns a high rate of particles that simulate rain drops, and despite not being visible in the project, also spawns water rings on the surface upon collision with the ground. The lightning in the background is also a particle system that utilizes noise to create a random variation to a straight line that simulates lightning. Upon birth, the lightning lets off a bright light at the top, and upon impact it also generates a bright light as well as sparks flying off the ground. The clouds of ShaderBender were made to look like volume rendered clouds, despite only being a shader on a simple plane. It was made this way to limit the computational power required, as the clouds are not a main feature of the game. It works by passing over a simple noise map, with added effects and some subdivisions to the plane to make it look a bit more 3D. The mountain in the background was made using the Blender plugin A.N.T Landscape, and was created to somewhat resemble Mt. Fuji. For the post-processing, effects such as bloom, vignette and white balance were utilized to create a night time environment. Lastly, the soundscape of the experience was created utilizing sounds from FreeSound.org, that were then mixed together using the software Audacity.

Goals and Motivation

Our goals when first drafting up what we wanted out of the project were as follows:

  • Create an engaging, open-ended interactive experience combining advanced visual and interaction techniques.
  • Utilise webcam-based motion capture as the primary input method, ensuring it is effective and engaging.
  • Enhance team expertise in shader programming, motion capture, and real-time rendering methods.

Our motivation for this project grew from the exciting opportunities it presented for exploration and learning. By creating an open-ended interactive experience without a traditional game loop, we aimed to develop something akin to an exhibit at an interactive museum - a space for creativity and discovery. The novelty of integrating motion capture, a relatively new area for our team, added an intriguing challenge that pushed us to expand our technical and design capabilities. In addition, the project offered a rich learning environment, encompassing diverse areas such as volume rendering, game character animation, motion capture techniques, ray marching, and shader programming.

Obstacles and Challenges

The project faced several technical and design challenges. The main hurdle early on was getting MediaPipe working within Unity, which complicated the integration of webcam-based gestures. The open-ended nature of the experience risked alienating users due to unclear interaction, which was a problem that further complicated the lack of recognisable gestures that we could track. Volume rendering in Unity was computationally intensive, requiring optimizations to balance performance and visual quality. Vertex displacement was a problem encountered during the last stretch of development, and time had to be spent optimising the effects to run on the GPU rather than CPU. Finally, achieving realistic environmental effects like smoke, weather, and day-night cycles posed challenges in maintaining aesthetic fidelity without exceeding computational limits.
